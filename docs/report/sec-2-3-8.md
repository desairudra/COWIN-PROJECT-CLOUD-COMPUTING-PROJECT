`sec-6-cost.md`. Sources are listed at the end as [B1], [B2] and so on.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 CoWIN

CoWIN (COVID Vaccine Intelligence Network) is the platform India used to register people for
COVID-19 vaccination, book slots, record each dose and issue certificates. It went live on
16 January 2021, the day the national vaccination programme began [B1]. By 6 January 2023 the
programme had given more than 220 crore doses, and between January 2021 and September 2022 the
platform held 104 crore beneficiaries [B2]. On 17 September 2021 it recorded 2.5 crore doses in
24 hours [B3].

### 2.2 The day the load arrived

The hardest day for registration was 28 April 2021, when registration opened at 4 pm for
everyone aged 18 to 44 ahead of their eligibility on 1 May. In the first three hours the
platform received 383 million API hits, at first up to 2.7 million a minute [B4], and recorded
55,000 hits a second at the peak [B5]. About 80 lakh people registered between 4 and 7 pm [B5],
and the day closed with 1.33 crore registrations and 2.78 crore SMS delivered [B6]. Users
reported errors in the first minutes, while the government said the system had not crashed and
was working as expected [B7].

Averaged over those three hours, that is

$$\frac{8\times10^{6}\ \text{registrations}}{3\times3600\ \text{s}}\approx 741\ \text{registrations per second},$$

the upper end of the 475 to 741 per second range this report designs and prices for.

### 2.3 Why this is a cloud computing problem

Four properties of that day shape everything in this report:

- **The surge was scheduled.** Everyone arrived at 4 pm because that is when registration
  opened. A system sized for an ordinary day falls over; a system sized for 4 pm every day is
  idle most of the time. This is the case for elastic capacity (Sections 5, 6 and E1).
- **Some rules need one source of truth.** At most four beneficiaries per mobile number, and
  each photo ID used once, across the whole country. Stateless servers scale easily; these
  rules do not, which is why the database design (Sec 4.6, commit 1, commit 16) matters most.
- **Every registration costs an SMS.** OTPs are the login, so bots that request OTPs cost money
  and capacity (E4, Sec 6.4).
- **Failures will happen during the peak.** Servers, zones and deployments fail or change while
  people are registering, so the platform has to keep answering through them (E2, E3, E5).

## 3. Solution overview

### 3.1 What we designed and what we built

We designed the registration side of a CoWIN-like platform for two AWS regions (Sections 4, 5
and 7, and the diagram in `docs/architecture/`), then built and measured the parts that can be
tested by a student team on one AWS account and a laptop.

| Part | Designed | Built and tested | Commits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Two regions, Route 53 failover | Mumbai + Hyderabad | Design only | 2 |
| Load balancer across Availability Zones | yes | ALB over ap-south-1a and 1b, health checks every 5 s | 4, 6 |
| Stateless app servers | yes | Lookup microservice on EC2 in two zones; registration service in containers | 3, 5, 11, 14 |
| Replace failed servers, scale with load | Auto Scaling | Auto Scaling group (CloudFormation) and Kubernetes HPA | 14, 15 |
| One source of truth for the rules | PostgreSQL, partitioned, sharded by mobile | Schema with the 4-per-mobile and unique photo ID rules; shard routing with a cross-shard photo ID claim | 1, 16 |
| Stop OTP abuse | WAF + per-mobile limits | Redis token bucket, OTP cooldown, attempt cap | 12 |
| See failures as users do | CloudWatch + probes | CloudWatch dashboard and alarms; a prober and live monitor page | 7, 8, 10 |
| Repeatable builds | Infrastructure as code | CLI scripts and one CloudFormation template; every run torn down after | 4-6, 15 |
| Cost | Pricing for 475-741 registrations/s | Cost model from our measured capacity; our real bill | 17 |

### 3.2 The system we ran

The TA's brief asked for the core of the design: a small service on two servers in two
Availability Zones behind a load balancer, which stays up when one zone is shut down. That is
what runs on AWS:

```
users / prober ──HTTP──▶ Application Load Balancer (both zones, /health every 5 s)
                              │                       │
                       ap-south-1a              ap-south-1b
                     lookup server            lookup server
                 (Auto Scaling group: at least 2, one per zone, replaced on failure)
```

Every answer names the server and zone that produced it (`served_by`), so a test from outside
can see exactly where each request went. The registration service (OTP, beneficiaries, rate
limits, sharded database) runs in Docker Compose and Kubernetes on a laptop, where the
experiments that need many restarts (E1 to E3, E6) are cheap to repeat.

## 8. Outcomes and lessons

### 8.1 What we measured

| Experiment | What we did | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| E5 failover, hard stop (commit 8) | Stopped the Zone A server while 5 requests/s hit the live site | **Stayed up.** 35 of 599 requests after the stop failed, all within 16 s; longest time without an answer 0.6 s |
| E5 failover, graceful (commit 8) | Drained Zone A first, then stopped it | **Stayed up.** 2 of 599 failed (client-side blips); longest gap 0.4 s |
| Load test, AWS (commit 13) | k6 from a laptop, steps up to 400 requests/s | Every step passed: 46,505 requests, 0 failed, split 50/50 between zones, p95 98 ms at the top |
| E4 OTP flood (commit 12) | Three kinds of bot plus 30 real users, limits off then on | With limits: 119 of 1,200 bot OTPs sent, 2 of 600 to the victim's phone, 6 guesses checked. Real users 30 of 30 both times |
| E1 autoscaling (commit 14) | 20 → 300 → 20 requests/s on Kubernetes | 2 → 8 pods, first scale-up 31 s after the ramp began, back to 2 pods 181 s after the drop; 0 of 71,000 failed |
| E2 pods killed (commit 14) | Force-killed a pod at 30, 60 and 90 s | 3,000 of 3,000 answered; longest gap 0.05 s |
| E2 zone down (commit 14) | Stopped the zone a node for 140 s | 5,199 of 5,200 answered; longest gap 0.10 s |
| E3 release (commit 14) | v1 → v2 while users were on the site | Rolling update: 2,000 of 2,000, longest gap 0.05 s. Recreate: a 9.2 s outage, 184 failed |
| Server replacement (commit 15) | Stopped a server in the Auto Scaling group | Replacement healthy about 100 s later with no one acting; 42 of 2,400 failed (1.75%), longest gap 0.6 s |
| E6 sharding (commit 16) | Same sign-up load on 1 table, 8 partitions, 2 and 4 databases (1 CPU each) | Sign-ups/s in two runs: 631/616, 456/380, 643/483, **1,731/1,813**. p95 per call fell from about 81 ms to 21 ms with 4 databases |
| Cost (commit 17) | Priced 475-741 registrations/s; read our own bill | Infrastructure about ₹0.007 per registration, SMS about ₹0.51. Our whole AWS bill: ₹0 (Free Tier) |

### 8.2 The brief

All five steps of the TA's brief were done on AWS and measured: the design (step 1), the
microservice (step 2), one server in each of two zones (step 3), the load balancer splitting
traffic evenly (step 4), and the failover test (step 5). The service stayed up through a hard
loss of Zone A, with a short burst of errors while the health checks noticed, and stayed up
without visible errors when the zone was drained first.

### 8.3 Lessons

- **Health checks decide how long a failure is visible.** After a hard stop the load balancer
  kept sending requests to the dead server for about 16 s (two failed checks, 5 s apart, plus
  timeouts), and that is where nearly all failed requests came from, on EC2 (E5, commit 15) and
  in Kubernetes alike. Draining before a planned stop removed them: 0.3% failed instead of 5.8%.
  Faster checks shorten the window but never close it, so planned work should always drain.
- **Redundancy needs automation to stay redundant.** In commit 8 the stopped server stayed
  stopped until someone relaunched it; in commit 15 the Auto Scaling group had a replacement
  serving in about 100 s. Two servers protect you from one failure; replacing failures protects
  you from the next one.
- **How you release matters as much as how many servers you have.** The same v2 release caused
  a 9.2 s outage when every pod was replaced at once, and nothing when pods were replaced one at
  a time after their replacements were ready (E3).
- **Partitioning organises data; sharding adds capacity, at a price.** On one server, eight
  partitions were 25-40% slower than one table (E6). Four separate databases gave about three
  times the throughput, but only because each had its own CPU; and keeping photo IDs unique across
  them needed an extra claim on a second database for every new beneficiary, and a question
  about everyone now has to ask every shard.
- **Rate limits protect the budget as well as the servers.** SMS is about 99% of the cost of a
  registration (Sec 6.4). Without limits, one bot machine triggered 1,200 OTP messages a
  minute; with them, 119 in the whole run, while every real user still got through (E4).
- **Measure from the user's side.** CloudWatch reports per minute; our probe recorded every
  request, which is what showed the 16 s window, the 0.6 s gaps and the one failed request in
  E2. Every experiment's verdict is "the longest time with no successful answer", which is what a
  user would notice.
- **Scripts made AWS cheap and safe to use.** Every setup was built by a script, used for an hour
  or two, and torn down by a script, so the whole project cost ₹0 and could be rebuilt for each
  experiment. Mistakes were cheap too: a missing permission or a stuck stack cost minutes, not money.
- **Testing found real bugs.** Our own runs exposed, among others: pods that ended up in one zone
  after a rolling update (the spread rule only applies when a pod is placed; fixed by counting
  only pods of the same version), a check that assumed capitalised HTTP headers, a test whose
  verdict rule would have failed a correct run, and a cost summary that claimed a reason for a
  ₹0 bill that was not the real one. Each was fixed and is in the commit history.

### 8.4 Limits of this work

- **One region built.** The Hyderabad region and Route 53 failover between regions are designed
  (Sec 4, 7) but were not built or tested.
- **The registration service did not run on AWS.** It was tested locally, in Docker Compose and in
  Kubernetes; on AWS we ran the lookup service. RDS, ElastiCache, WAF and SQS appear in the design
  and the cost model, not in our account.
- **No real SMS.** OTPs were returned to the page (demo mode); DLT registration and an SMS
  provider would be needed before launch.
- **Laptop-sized load.** The heaviest test was 400 requests/s against AWS and about 1,800
  sign-ups/s in E6, from one laptop that was also running the databases. Reaching 55,000 hits a
  second needs distributed load generators.
- **No HTTPS.** The load balancer listened on HTTP only; a real deployment needs a certificate
  (ACM) and redirects to HTTPS.
- **Prices change.** Section 6 uses November 2026 prices, some from other regions, and should be
  checked in the AWS Pricing Calculator before use.

### 8.5 If we continued

Deploy the registration service with RDS and ElastiCache through the CloudFormation stack; build
the Hyderabad region and test a regional failover with Route 53; put WAF in front with a rate
rule; run the load from several machines; and add a clean-up job for photo ID claims left behind
by a crash between the claim and the insert (Sec 4.6, commit 16).
